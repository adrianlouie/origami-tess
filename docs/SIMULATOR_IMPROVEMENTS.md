# Improvements drawn from the simulation references

A running log, in the style of `docs/BOOK_IMPROVEMENTS.md`. Written 2026-11-15 after a full read of three sources the user supplied. Nothing here is a plan step until it has an entry in `docs/PLAN.md`; each item names the step that takes it up.

Tags: **[sim]** solver or physics, **[ui]** viewer controls and look, **[import]** reading files, **[export]** writing files, **[math]** a formula or check, **[doc]** a wording or citation fix. Priority: P1 do soon, P2 useful, P3 later or optional.

## Sources

1. **Schenk and Guest**, "Origami Folding: A Structural Engineering Approach" (5OSME, 2010; 16 pages). A partly folded sheet as a pin-jointed truss: a bar on every fold line and triangle edge, plus dihedral-angle constraints, analysed by linear algebra.
2. **Tachi**, "Freeform Variations of Origami", _Journal for Geometry and Graphics_ 14(2), 2010, pp. 203–215. A design system that deforms a known pattern in 3D while keeping developability and flat-foldability.
3. **Ghassaei's Origami Simulator** (`github.com/amandaghassaei/OrigamiSimulator`, MIT licence, cloned and read in full: `js/`, the shaders in `index.html`, the importer and exporters, about 9 200 lines). It builds on 1 and 2 and is the source of the screenshots the user wants the viewer to resemble. We take ideas and formulas and write our own code; the licence would also allow copying, but the repo is plain JavaScript with global state, so there is nothing to lift. Its example SVG files have no separate licence statement, so we do not copy them either.

## What we already have, confirmed by the read

- `core/simulate/relax.ts` is the same method as the simulator's dynamic solver: Newton's second law with a viscous term, explicit symplectic Euler, a fold percentage scaling every target crease angle. Our defaults (axial 20, crease 0.7, damping 0.45) match its `globals.js`; our `dt` has the same 1 / (2π ω) form.
- Our hinge gradient (`dihedral.ts`, ∂ρ/∂x = n/h with a lever rule) is Tachi's Eqs. 15–18 and Schenk and Guest's Eq. 5 in another form.
- Developability (Tachi Eq. 1, zero Gauss area at an interior vertex) is our `angleDefect`; Kawasaki and the angular inequality Tachi calls Eq. 6 (θᵢ ≥ min(θᵢ₋₁, θᵢ₊₁) for two equal creases) are our Kawasaki and Big-Little-Big. His "empirical" Eqs. 7 and 8 are not needed; our exact layer solver decides.
- Our `U` and `F` and `B` assignments are FOLD's `U`, `F`, `B`; M and V match too. Only FOLD's `C` (cut) is missing, on purpose: the project is uncut paper only.
- The simulator has no self-collision, no check that a pattern is flat-foldable and no idea where a pattern fails. We have exact validators, a layer solver and a conflict locator. The differentiator is to show that verdict next to the simulation.

## Findings about our own code

1. **[sim] P1: a collapsed hinge throws in ours and is switched off in the simulator.** `foldAngle` and `foldAngleGradient` throw `RangeError` when a hinge axis has zero length or a wing node lies on the axis. The simulator (`updateCreaseGeo` shader) marks such a crease disabled (length or wing height below 1e-6) and skips its force, so a hard pull or a bad pattern keeps running. Grab mode (V.2b) will reach this state, and `main.ts` currently stops the loop on the exception. Take the simulator's approach: a degenerate hinge contributes no force, and the model reports how many are disabled. Tested with a hand-built degenerate triangle and with a Grab pull.
2. **[sim] P1: our `facet` default may be the wrong constant.** The `DEFAULT_PARAMS` comment says our `facet` 0.2 is the simulator's `faceStiffness`. In the simulator three constants exist: `creaseStiffness` 0.7 (M and V hinges, times length), **`panelStiffness` 0.7** (the flat diagonal hinges the triangulation adds, times length) and **`faceStiffness` 0.2**, which multiplies the angle error of every triangle corner (an angle spring, with no length factor). So our facet hinge corresponds to `panelStiffness` 0.7, and 0.2 belongs to our `angle` spring, whose scale (20) is ours. Decide in V.2c whether to match the simulator (facet 0.7, angle 0.2 with its force form) or keep our values and correct the comment; either way re-run the settling tests at every gadget and fold.
3. **[sim] P2: creases are undamped in the simulator, damping is per beam.** Its crease damping is commented out in the shader; each bar has D = ζ · 2√(K · m). We use one global coefficient c = 2ζ√(k_soft · m) for a reason recorded in `model.ts` (per-node critical damping took more than 240 000 steps). No change; recorded so nobody "fixes" the difference.
4. **[doc] P3: the simulator keeps positions as offsets from the rest position** (`lastPosition + originalPosition`) because the GPU is 32-bit. We use 64-bit floats, so the trick is not needed on the CPU; it matters only if relaxation moves to a GPU.

## Improvements

### Viewer and controls (V.1, V.2a, V.2b)

1. **[ui] P1 Strain view (V.2a).** The simulator's exact recipe, as a pure tested function. Per node, strain is the mean over its bars of |length / rest length − 1| (a fraction, shown as a percent); the colour is HSL with hue 0.7 · (1 − min(strain, clip) / clip), saturation 1, lightness 0.5, where `clip` is 5 percent (blue at zero, red at the clip). A global error readout is the mean node strain. A second mode measures angle strain: the mean over a node's triangle corners of |nominal − current| / nominal. We already compute bar strain in `maxStrain`.
2. **[ui] P1 Material view (V.2a).** Front colour `#ec008b`, back `#dddddd`, both user-changeable (the user asked for that); flat shading; the front and back are two meshes (front-face and back-face materials) so the back shows grey when the paper is turned over, and the lines are drawn with a polygon offset so they do not z-fight with the sheet. Six directional lights (two strong from above and the sides, weaker from below and the ends), no ambient term. A "normal" colour mode (mesh normal material) is a cheap third option.
3. **[ui] P1 Visibility toggles (V.2a).** Edges, mountains, valleys, facet (triangulation) lines, passive (unassigned) lines, the border, and the mesh itself, each on its own switch. Defaults: edges, mountains, valleys, border on; facet and passive off.
4. **[ui] P1 Camera presets (V.1).** Views along ±x, ±y, ±z and an isometric view, plus auto-rotate about an axis with a speed setting. The simulator uses a trackball (rotate speed 4, no pan, zoom limits 1 to 30); we keep our own rig and add pan. The model is scaled to a unit bounding sphere and centred, and it is re-centred after a drag so it does not drift off screen.
5. **[ui] P1 Grab mode (V.2b).** The simulator's method: raycast the mesh, take the nearest of the three corners of the hit triangle, pin that node while the pointer is down, move it to the intersection of the pointer ray with the plane through the node that faces the camera, and on release unpin it, re-centre the model and zero the velocities. Orbit is disabled during a drag.
6. **[ui] P2 Fold percent from −100 to 100 (V.2a, V.2c).** Negative values fold with every mountain and valley swapped. Today our slider runs from 0 to 98 (`MAX_FOLD`, to avoid z-fighting of coplanar layers). Keep the cap on the folded side. The Flat and Folded labels sit under the slider ends, as in the screenshots.
7. **[ui] P1 Show the foldability verdict, which the simulator cannot (V.2a).** The Pattern tab marks the creases of the `mvConflict` or `nearFoldability` result in a warning colour and says "flat-foldable" or "not flat-foldable here" in words. A later flat-folded preview (D.2) shows the layer order from `layered-view.ts`.
8. **[ui] P3 Simulation controls (V.2c, optional).** Pause, resume, single step, reset, the stiffness and damping parameters, steps per frame (the simulator's default is 100) and a "delta T" readout, all in an "Advanced options" panel. Euler or Verlet integration is a switch in the simulator; we do not need Verlet.

### Solver (V.2c)

9. **[sim] P1 Disable collapsed hinges (finding 1).**
10. **[sim] P1 One-sided angle limits (Tachi §2.4).** A crease cannot flip to the wrong side during smooth folding: a mountain's angle ρ must stay in [0, π] and a valley's in [−π, 0] (his sign convention), and a flat diagonal's in [−απ, απ] with 0 ≤ α < 1 as the paper's flexibility (he uses 0.1 for Miura-ori). He enforces it as the penalty max(g, 0)². Our 7.3c finding is exactly this failure: the full star twist has an M crease on the wrong side up to fold 0.5 (edges 41 and 43), and the partial star's conflict shows as wrong-side creases (edges 2 and 26). A one-sided penalty is the first thing to try for the star twist that "does not look right" (V.6). Test: the star cases above end with every M and V crease on its own side at fold 0.5, and no gadget that settled before settles differently.
11. **[sim] P1 Per-crease target angles (partial folds).** A crease may carry its own fraction of a full fold: the SVG importer reads it from stroke opacity (angle = opacity · 180°), FOLD carries `edges_foldAngle`. Today every crease asks for the same ±fold · π. `HingeSpring` gets a target, the importer fills it, the default stays 1.
12. **[sim] P2 Move the relaxation off the main thread.** Our CPU loop spends a 10 ms budget per frame and the largest gadget needs about 45 000 steps from flat. A Web Worker keeps the page responsive and is a small change on the `FoldDriver` boundary. A GPU port (the simulator packs positions, velocities, angles and neighbour lists into floating-point textures and runs the force pass in a fragment shader; it caps a node at 100 bars or creases) is only worth it if profiling at the sizes V.3 and V.4 produce says the worker is too slow. Not planned.
13. **[sim] P2 Initial pose continuity.** Its dihedral angle tracks the previous angle and adds or subtracts 2π when it jumps by more than 5 rad. Our `angles` state already unwraps across ±π; no change, but test a full fold and back.

### Import and export (V.5, V.9)

14. **[import] P1 SVG convention (V.5).** Stroke colour gives the assignment: black is the border, red a mountain, blue a valley, green a cut, yellow a triangulation (flat) line, magenta a passive (unassigned) hinge; opacity is the fold fraction. Unknown colours are collected and listed to the user. We accept the same convention, **reject green** with a clear message (uncut paper only), treat yellow as `F` and magenta as `U`, and make our SVG export optionally use these exact colours so a file round-trips with Origami Simulator. Our own styles stay the default (dash patterns survive black and white; see `crease-style.ts`).
15. **[import] P1 The pipeline that makes an arbitrary drawing a pattern (V.5).** Order, from `pattern.js`: collect segments by type; merge vertices closer than a tolerance (the simulator's default is 3 drawing units); split edges where they cross; merge collinear and remove stray vertices; find the faces; triangulate (earcut and cdt2d). For us the pipeline ends differently: after the tolerance merge and the crossing split, **snap every vertex to the lattice or reject the file with the nearest-miss distance**, then `fromSegments` builds the pattern. The tolerance is one named constant, with the import dialog able to change it, as in the simulator.
16. **[import] P1 FOLD import (V.5).** Required keys: `vertices_coords`, `edges_vertices`, `edges_assignment`, `faces_vertices`; anything missing is a named error. Spec 1.0 files use `edges_foldAngles`; accept it as `edges_foldAngle`. When there is no fold angle, the simulator offers to derive angles from a 3D file by the angle between the two face normals (negative for a mountain); we only import crease patterns, so a flat file takes full ±180° and a 3D file is rejected with the reason.
17. **[import] P2 Convenience (V.5).** Drag and drop a file onto the page, and paste SVG text from the clipboard (Illustrator and Cuttle.xyz copy SVG as text). The simulator's `postMessage` embedding API (`importFold`, `importSVG`) is skipped.
18. **[export] P1 FOLD export (V.5).** FOLD 1.1: `file_spec`, `file_creator`, `frame_classes` (`creasePattern` for the flat pattern, `foldedForm` for a simulated state), `frame_attributes` (`2D` or `3D`), `frame_unit`, the four required arrays and `edges_foldAngle`. Optional triangulated export. This is the round-trip partner of item 16 and the acceptance test for V.5.
19. **[export] P2 Capture (V.9).** PNG of the view, GIF and WebM of a fold animation, with the simulator's sequence editor idea: an ordered list of items, each an animation to a fold percent over a duration or a delay. Our fold steps (`core/pattern/fold-steps.ts`) are a natural source for the default sequence.
20. **[export] P2 3D export (V.9).** STL and OBJ of the folded state, single or double-sided, an export scale, and an optional thickened model (an offset along the normals) so the file prints as a solid.
21. **[export] P3.** Save the pattern as SVG (the simulator's colour convention, item 14) and a vector screenshot of the folded model. We already export SVG and PDF of the flat pattern.

### Analysis (V.10, after B.0)

22. **[math] P1 Rigid-mechanism count (Schenk and Guest §3.2).** Stack the bar compatibility matrix C (bar extensions per node displacement) with the fold-angle Jacobian J (Eq. 5, dθ per displacement); the nullspace of [C; J] holds the displacements that stretch no bar and keep every dihedral angle: the rigid origami mechanisms. Its dimension is the number of rigid degrees of freedom, and the SVD of the stacked matrix computes it. For a Miura-ori or an Eggbox sheet it is one (the paper's Fig. 6, a single planar mechanism). Use as a rigid-foldability test oracle for D.1 and as a number the UI can show ("rigidly foldable, 1 degree of freedom" or "not rigid: the panels must bend"). Needs the square lattice (B.0) for the two oracles; the triangle twists, which are known not rigid (Lang §7.6.1), work today as the negative cases.
23. **[math] P2 Stiffness eigenmodes (§3.3, Eqs. 7–9).** K = CᵀGC extended by the angle constraints, with the axial stiffness several orders above the bending stiffness; the softest eigenmodes are the sheet's natural ways to deform (twist, saddle, spherical, cylindrical). They can be drawn as an animation of the mode shape. K_ratio = K_facet / K_fold is the one dimensionless material parameter (about 1 for paper, plastic and metal; K_ratio → ∞ is rigid panels on frictionless hinges); the softest modes stay the same over a wide range of it, which says the behaviour comes from the geometry. A K_ratio slider with a log-log eigenvalue plot is the paper's Figs. 7 and 8.
24. **[math] P3 Coordinate change dθ = T d (§3.4).** Express a mode in fold-angle changes, which origamists read more easily than node displacements; T is the J of item 22.
25. **[math] P3 Pseudo-inverse path following (Tachi Eqs. 9–10, Schenk and Guest §3.2).** Follow a mechanism by Euler steps along the nullspace, dx = (I − J⁺J) dx₀, and a Newton–Raphson correction to remove the drift. A rigid-folding animation for the viewer (D.1), as an alternative to the relaxation.

### Preset library and generators (V.3, V.7)

26. **[doc] P1 More presets from Tachi §6.** Resch's triangular pattern (§6.4, developable, not flat-foldable, a tuck hidden behind a plane; on the triangular lattice, so exact), the Yoshimura pattern (§6.3, all vertices convex, so its curvature is always positive), the waterbomb tessellation (§6.1), the Miura-ori (§6.2, square lattice, V.8). The plan's "optional side modules" already list them; V.3 can ship the triangular-lattice ones first. The simulator's example menu also has the hypar, a single square twist and a square twist at many angles (square lattice), and several Lang patterns; its `CreasePatternScripts/` (Processing sketches for a square twist, a Resch tessellation, a hypar and a triangle tessellation) show how it makes them.
27. **[sim] P3 A "crumpled paper" preset.** Tachi §6.5: a plain triangular mesh with no creases, deformed under the developability condition, looks like crumpled paper and avoids the regular triangle artefacts that direct non-stretching simulation shows. A fun example for the viewer; needs the freeform deformation of item 29.
28. **[math] P2 Edge-collapse generator move (Tachi §4).** A short edge is collapsed to merge two vertices, with three rules that keep the flat-folded state unchanged: the merged edge's target angle is the sum of the two, the angles around the merged vertex must sum to ±2π (Maekawa), and an interior vertex keeps at least four creases. Two cases are allowed: two ±2π vertices merged by collapsing a ±π edge, or a 2π and a −2π vertex. On the lattice this is exact (the merged vertex is one of the two endpoints). A move for `core/generate/` next to the splice and subdivision moves already in the back-log (BOOK_IMPROVEMENTS §8.3), and it changes the pattern's topology, which gives new combinations of vertex types (his Fig. 7).
29. **[sim] P3, needs a decision: freeform editing (Tachi §5).** Drag a vertex in the folded 3D state while the system keeps developability, the boundary conditions (Eqs. 2–4: sector angles and edge lengths on the perimeter preserved, or the boundary convex) and, if wanted, flat-foldability. Mathematically it is dx = (I − J⁺J) dx₀ for the user's drag dx₀, with a Newton–Raphson correction at every step. Tachi reports 1 to 5 frames per second for 256 vertices with 452 constraints. This produces **float** patterns that are generally not on the lattice, so it cannot enter `pattern/` and the validators under the B.0 decision (floats are snapped first). Options for later: keep it as a float-only design mode that exports FOLD and never feeds `core/validate`, or snap its result. Recorded, not planned.

## Deliberately not adopted

- **Cut edges** (the simulator's green lines, `splitCuts`): the project is uncut paper only.
- **Curved folding** (Ghassaei's `curvedFolding.js`, 2 900 lines, ruling-aware triangulation; Tachi §6.6, which found jagged surfaces): out of scope for a lattice-based tool.
- **VR, the static and rigid solver modes**: the static solver is a linear solve with fixed nodes and applied forces, and its rigid solver is an unfinished stub in the repository. Item 22 is the rigorous version of the rigid mode.
- **Tachi's Origamizer comparison and crimp folds** (his §1): outside the project.
